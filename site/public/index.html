The idea

A cairn is a stack of stones that marks a trail: placed by hand, built to last, and trusted by whoever comes next. Here the trails are a product's flows, and the stones are claims about them: “pressing ^12 clears the completed items”, “^12's button is visible and on top whenever there's something to clear”. Each claim is backed by a Lean proof, a model check or a conformance run against the real app.

To make those claims mean something, the code they talk about has to be the code that runs. So the app's state, view, layout, hit testing, focus and text editing are all pure Lean, and the theorems are about exactly those functions.

Not a layer on top

Cairn isn't verification bolted onto an existing UI framework. It is its own framework: the app, its view, layout, hit testing, focus and text editing are written in Lean, and a thin Swift host hands over events and draws the result with Metal.

Owning every layer is what lets a claim reach all the way down. Each one is bound to the code that actually runs, not to a model of someone else's, and the little we do have to trust, the host, is written down in one place.

What a claim is

A claim is one statement about the app, in two halves, and both live in the app's source under one id. The human half says what a person does and what follows. Because the update is built only from moves, two things hold for every app, proved once in the framework: an action that no claim owns changes nothing and asks nothing of the host (update_unclaimed), and every effect the app does ask for comes from a move (update_effect).

Ids are the contract

^2 means Reset for as long as the counter exists. Ids are never renumbered or reused: a claim that goes away is retired with a reason, and its id stays issued. claims.lock records every id ever issued with a hash of its formal statement. The build fails when an id vanishes without being retired, comes back after it, or changes kind, and it warns when a statement's meaning changes, because a new meaning needs a new id. A control that repeats once per item carries a key: ^5#12 is ^5 for item 12. Ids are scoped by package: Cairn's own are cairn^N, and an app in its own repository has its own, like Yap's yap^311.

How sure we are

Every claim carries its evidence, and the map shows which kind. Strongest first:

  • Proved. Lean's kernel checked the proof, using only the three standard axioms; make check audits every theorem.
  • Conformance-checked. The real app, driven through generated sequences of moves, agrees with the model after every step.
  • Assumed. Recorded, never proved. Theorems that rely on it take it as a hypothesis, so every use shows.
  • Seen. A screenshot of the real app with the claim's control pinned on it.

The map turns this into pages: every claim an app makes, with its evidence and screenshots of the real app, generated from the build and nothing written by hand. See the maps for the counter, Basics, TodoMVC and the trusted base.
